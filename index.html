Chapter 01

Windows 98, age four or so

I was three or four when we got our first computer at home. Windows 98, a CRT monitor that buzzed before the picture showed up, and a beige tower that took forever to boot. I had no idea what anything was called. I remember sitting way too close to the screen, clicking on everything, amazed that the thing actually did what I asked. Sometimes it didn't. That was even more interesting, because I wanted to know why.

I'm still that kid poking at the machine. The tools just got better.

Chapter 02

Cracked games and forum threads

I grew up on games. The PC, a Family Game, a Sega. I loved strategy games, the ones you lose twenty times before they click. Games were expensive in Argentina, so like a lot of kids I went looking for cracks for Mu Online, World of Warcraft, Counter Strike and Age of Empires. Half of them didn't work. So I read forum threads, readme files and very long posts from people who knew way more than me, trying to figure out why one patch worked and the next one didn't. I'm not proud of the piracy. I am grateful for the habit: when something breaks, go find out why.

That's still how I debug.

Chapter 03

Ports, routers and a lot of questions

The more cracks failed, the more I wanted to know how games talked to their servers. Why did this one need a port open? What was my firewall blocking? What even is a server? I started reading about TCP, packets and protocols, mostly to understand words I kept seeing on forums. At some point the questions got more interesting than the games. I could picture my PC, the router, the firewall and some server far away, and how a message traveled between them.

I came for the games and stayed for the plumbing.

Chapter 04

Sixteen, and one weird login

At sixteen I spent hours on Mu Online and Lineage private server sites. On one of them I found ’ OR 1=1 -- in a forum post. I copied it, pasted it into the login box, and I was in. Then I started changing it myself, and soon I was getting the in-game items I wanted. I'm not proud of that part. But I didn't stop until I understood why it worked: the site pasted whatever you typed straight into its SQL query, and 1=1 is always true. That stuck with me. Today I validate every input.

I write code assuming a sixteen-year-old me is on the other side.

Chapter 07

Today

My last role taught me something I keep repeating: AI agents do much better work when they get a spec and tests before writing any code. So I built ArggonManager around that idea. Tasks live as Markdown files in the repo, and agents pick them up, code them and review each other through git, the same way people do. I keep the decisions you can't undo, like accepting a feature or cutting a release. I try it on small side projects, and this site is one of them. On weekends I'm learning Rust. Coming from C#, the compiler is strict with me, and I like that.

If you want to see how I work, the code is on GitHub.
